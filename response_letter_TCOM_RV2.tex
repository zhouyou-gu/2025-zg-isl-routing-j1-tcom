\section*{\centering\Large\textbf{Response to Reviewer 1}}

\noindent{\bfseries\pendingcomment Thanks for the revision. The authors have addressed my major concerns. A few issues still need clarification.}

We thank the reviewer for the careful assessment and constructive comments. We will clarify the ATP replay and the practical timing limits of centralized deployment in the responses that follow.

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 1.1: The ATP study uses 10 s topology intervals and TATP values up to 30 s, while the manuscript cites optical acquisition times on the order of tens of seconds. Why is this parameter range sufficient, and what happens when TATP exceeds the topology interval for several consecutive snapshots?}

\textbf{Response:} The original range covered acquisition over two or three topology intervals, but did not test longer delays. We therefore extended Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_atp_transition} to include $50$, $70$, and $90$ s, covering up to nine intervals. When $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$ exceeds the $10$ s interval, a newly selected terminal pair remains unavailable while acquisition progresses across snapshots. It becomes usable after $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$ if continuously retained; dropping and reselecting it restarts acquisition. Thus, frequent link changes can prevent acquisition from completing and reduce throughput, as Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_atp_transition} shows. The evaluation uses a $360$ s replay with pre-acquired initial links and excludes the initial snapshot from the averages.

\textbf{Manuscript changes:} In Section~\ref{sec:simulation}, under ``Impact of ATP Time,'' we revised the evaluation description and discussion as

``...\blue{To evaluate non-zero ATP overhead without replacing the proposed per-snapshot optimizer, we perform an ATP-time sensitivity simulation on the single-shell-based Starlink instance. The topology-evaluation interval is fixed to $10$ s over $0$--$360$ s, and the ATP acquisition time is swept from $0$ to $90$ s.
For each method, we first compute the original per-snapshot matching, routing, and rate allocation once for each offset. We then replay the ATP transition process for each value of $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$.
A LISL newly selected at snapshot $t$ is unavailable over $[t,t+T_{\mathrm{ATP}})$, and it becomes usable once the acquisition time has elapsed. Acquisition progress is carried across snapshots while the same terminal pair remains selected. If the pair is dropped before acquisition completes, its ATP state is discarded, and reselection starts a new acquisition. Finally, we recompute the throughput by LP using only the ATP-completed LISLs.
The results in Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_atp_transition} show that increasing ATP acquisition time can materially reduce realized throughput when the per-snapshot topology changes quickly.
The retention ratios quantify how much of the original per-snapshot throughput remains when ATP overhead is considered. Methods that change many LISLs across nearby snapshots have lower retention ratios as $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$ increases, whereas methods with more persistent LISLs are less sensitive. These results characterize the tested replay window rather than long-run performance for arbitrary acquisition times. These results motivate the future ATP-aware temporal optimization described in the appendix, complementing recent time-dependent topology optimization studies for LEO constellations \cite{ron2025time}.}''

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 1.2: Computing time still reaches minutes for larger constellations, but the system model presents a central controller operating per snapshot. Will the dissemination delay and solver time fit the intended control interval once ATP overhead and traffic prediction updates are included? You\textquotesingle{}d better discuss this deployment issue more concretely.}

\textbf{Response:} The current implementation does not establish that the complete controller cycle fits within a seconds-scale update interval. For larger constellations, optimization already takes minutes, before adding state collection, traffic-prediction updates, dissemination, and ATP execution. Advance planning is a possible deployment option, conditional on sufficient lead time, applicable predicted inputs, and continued link feasibility during acquisition and service. Invalidating disturbances require refreshed state and recomputation. Further research can accelerate the approximation of optimal Lagrange multipliers using duality-guided graph learning (DeepLaDu), which estimates the multipliers in a single forward pass of an offline-trained graph neural network \cite{gu2026deepladu}. This approach can replace iterative online dual updates while retaining the subsequent matching, routing, and rate-allocation steps. We added the timing limitation, the associated deployment conditions, and this direction for further research to the computing-time discussion.

\textbf{Manuscript changes:} In Section~\ref{sec:simulation}, under ``Evaluation of Computational Complexity,'' we added a paragraph explaining the deployment timing limitation and the conditions for advance planning, together with the graph-learning acceleration approach, as

``...\blue{These measurements cover dual optimization and decision conversion. The minutes-scale optimization time for larger constellations does not support seconds-scale controller updates, even before including state collection, traffic-prediction updates, schedule dissemination, and ATP execution. Further research can accelerate the approximation of optimal Lagrange multipliers using duality-guided graph learning (DeepLaDu), which uses an offline-trained graph neural network to estimate the multipliers in a single forward pass \cite{gu2026deepladu}. This approach can replace iterative online dual updates while retaining the subsequent matching, routing, and rate-allocation steps. Advance planning would require sufficient lead time and predicted inputs that remain applicable at the intended execution epoch, with selected links remaining connectable throughout acquisition and service. Unexpected failures or traffic changes that invalidate a schedule require refreshed state and recomputation.}''

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 2.1: The structured‑shell test uses only one 1000‑satellite block from a single Starlink shell at a fixed epoch. Real mega‑constellations involve multiple shells, orbital precession, and time‑varying link geometry. Testing on a single static snapshot limits the generality of the conclusions.}

\textbf{Response:} The structured-shell evaluation was not limited to a single static snapshot. In the original evaluation, now shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_shell_time_baselines}, we kept the identities of the $1000$ selected satellites fixed and propagated their orbits to offsets of $0$, $30$, $60$, $90$, $120$, and $180$ min from $\mathcal{T}_0$. The original evaluation nevertheless used satellites from only one shell. We kept this selected population fixed and applied the same six-snapshot evaluation, using matched geometry, traffic, and gateway inputs across methods at each time.
DuJo achieves the highest throughput and served-demand ratio at all six snapshot times in both configurations.

\noindent{\bfseries\pendingcomment Comment 3.7: In fig 8 throughput does monotonically increase with FOR, which seems to have some explanation. Please provide some inputs on this issue.}

\textbf{Response:} For fixed satellite geometry, terminal orientations, link-rate parameters, and demand, increasing the field-of-regard half-angle relaxes the visibility constraints and enlarges the set of candidate links. A solution feasible at a smaller angle remains feasible at a larger angle, because additional links need not be selected. Thus, the optimal throughput cannot decrease under these matched conditions, although it may saturate and need not grow linearly. The observed gain is consistent with additional matching and routing alternatives that relieve bottlenecks. We will distinguish this feasible-set argument from a guarantee about every output of DuJo's finite-iteration procedure or its baselines, whose heuristic decisions need not be monotone.
