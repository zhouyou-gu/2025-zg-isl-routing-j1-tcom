\section*{\centering\Large\textbf{Response to the Editor's Note}}

We thank the editor for identifying the principal issues requiring attention and for the opportunity to address them in this revision.

\noindent{\bfseries Comment E.1: All reviewers still have notable suggestions on this paper, particularly in the performance evaluation part. Therefore, I recommend that you update your paper and submit a revised version for further processing.}

\textbf{Response:} We revised the manuscript to address the reviewers' remaining concerns, particularly those regarding performance evaluation. The main changes are summarized below.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Performance evaluation.} We extended the ATP sensitivity study to acquisition times of up to $90$ s, added a two-shell case evaluated at multiple snapshot times, and added a gateway-count comparison. The appendix now compares flow propagation delays and installed LCT counts across DuJo and the baseline methods.
  \item \textbf{Modeling and deployment.} We clarified acquisition-timer behavior, the timing limitations of centralized deployment, the aggregate traffic and payload assumptions, and the interpretation of served-demand ratios. We also explained the effects of LCT availability and FOR, including the nonmonotonic throughput of approximate solutions.
  \item \textbf{Presentation and related work.} We enlarged figure fonts and bars, added the FOR half-angle to the system diagram, clarified the distinction from related routing methods, and proofread the manuscript.
\end{itemize}

The detailed point-by-point responses to the reviewers are listed as follows.

\section*{\centering\Large\textbf{Response to Reviewer 1}}

We thank the reviewer for the constructive comments, which helped us extend the ATP sensitivity study and clarify acquisition behavior and the practical timing limits of centralized deployment.

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 1.1: The ATP study uses 10 s topology intervals and TATP values up to 30 s, while the manuscript cites optical acquisition times on the order of tens of seconds. Why is this parameter range sufficient, and what happens when TATP exceeds the topology interval for several consecutive snapshots?}

\textbf{Response:} The original range covered acquisition over two or three topology intervals, but did not test longer delays. We therefore extended Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_atp_transition} to include $50$, $70$, and $90$ s, covering up to nine intervals. When $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$ exceeds the $10$ s interval, a newly selected terminal pair remains unavailable while acquisition progresses across snapshots. It becomes usable after $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$ if continuously retained; dropping and reselecting it restarts acquisition. Thus, frequent link changes can prevent acquisition from completing and reduce throughput, as Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_atp_transition} shows. The evaluation uses a $360$ s replay with pre-acquired initial links and excludes the initial snapshot from the averages.
Overall, longer acquisition times reduce throughput, with methods that retain links across snapshots being less affected. This highlights the importance of accounting for link persistence alongside per-snapshot throughput optimization.

\textbf{Manuscript changes:} In Section~\ref{sec:simulation}, under ``Impact of ATP Time,'' we revised the evaluation description and discussion as

``...\blue{To evaluate non-zero ATP overhead without replacing the proposed per-snapshot optimizer, we perform an ATP-time sensitivity simulation on the single-shell Starlink instance. The topology-evaluation interval is fixed at $10$ s over $0$--$360$ s, and the ATP acquisition time is swept from $0$ to $90$ s.
For each method, we first compute the original per-snapshot matching, routing, and rate allocation once for each offset. We then replay the ATP transition process for each value of $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$.
A LISL newly selected at snapshot $t$ is unavailable over $[t,t+T_{\mathrm{ATP}})$, and it becomes usable once the acquisition time has elapsed. Acquisition progress is carried across snapshots while the same terminal pair remains selected. If the pair is dropped before acquisition completes, its ATP state is discarded, and reselection starts a new acquisition. Finally, we recompute the throughput by LP using only the ATP-completed LISLs.
The results in Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_atp_transition} show that increasing ATP acquisition time can materially reduce realized throughput when the per-snapshot topology changes quickly.
The retention ratios quantify how much of the original per-snapshot throughput remains when ATP overhead is considered. Methods that change many LISLs across nearby snapshots have lower retention ratios as $T_{\mathrm{ATP}}$ increases, whereas methods with more persistent LISLs are less sensitive. These results characterize the tested replay window rather than long-run performance for arbitrary acquisition times. They also motivate the future ATP-aware temporal optimization described in Appendix~\ref{appendix:mdp_extension}, complementing recent time-dependent topology optimization studies for LEO constellations \cite{ron2025time}.}''

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 1.2: Computing time still reaches minutes for larger constellations, but the system model presents a central controller operating per snapshot. Will the dissemination delay and solver time fit the intended control interval once ATP overhead and traffic prediction updates are included? You\textquotesingle{}d better discuss this deployment issue more concretely.}

\textbf{Response:} The current implementation does not establish that the complete controller cycle fits within a seconds-scale update interval. For larger constellations, optimization already takes minutes, before adding state collection, traffic-prediction updates, dissemination, and ATP execution. Advance planning is a possible deployment option, conditional on sufficient lead time, applicable predicted inputs, and continued link feasibility during acquisition and service. Invalidating disturbances require refreshed state and recomputation. Further research can investigate learning-based approximation using graph neural networks to compute the multipliers with reduced online computation, while retaining the subsequent matching, routing, and rate-allocation steps. We added the timing limitation, the associated deployment conditions, and this direction for further research to the computing-time discussion.

\textbf{Manuscript changes:} In Section~\ref{sec:simulation}, under ``Evaluation of Computational Complexity,'' we added a paragraph explaining the deployment timing limitation and the conditions for advance planning, together with the graph-learning acceleration approach, as

``...\blue{These measurements cover dual optimization and decision conversion. The minutes-scale optimization time for larger constellations does not support seconds-scale controller updates, even before including state collection, traffic-prediction updates, schedule dissemination, and ATP execution. Further research can investigate learning-based approximation using graph neural networks to compute the multipliers with reduced online computation, while retaining the subsequent matching, routing, and rate-allocation steps. Advance planning would require sufficient lead time and predicted inputs that remain applicable at the intended execution epoch, with selected links remaining connectable throughout acquisition and service. Unexpected failures or traffic changes that invalidate a schedule require refreshed state and recomputation.}''

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 3.2: Served demand does not reach 50\%. Authors are requested to explain if it is due to mismatch of simulation parameters (e.g. user demand vs capacity of ISLs), ISL bottlenecks or due to the proposed optimization.}

\textbf{Response:} The fundamental limitation is that gateway capacity can serve remote demand only through feasible LISL routes. Limited LCT availability and steering ranges restrict these routes, and their link capacities constrain the traffic they can carry. Consequently, the selected topologies cannot deliver enough traffic to satisfy all residual demand, preventing the served-demand ratio from reaching $100\%$ in the evaluated cases. Reducing the active-user percentage lowers demand but does not create missing routes between gateway-serving and demanding satellites, so unreachable demand remains unserved. The nearly unchanged served-demand ratio at lighter loads in Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_tcom_shell_time_load_scaling} suggests that connectivity is a key bottleneck rather than traffic intensity alone.
Increasing the number of LCTs per satellite or the number of satellites can improve connectivity and increase throughput and the served-demand ratio for a fixed traffic demand.

\noindent{\bfseries Comment 3.7: In fig 8 throughput does monotonically increase with FOR, which seems to have some explanation. Please provide some inputs on this issue.}

\textbf{Response:} A wider FOR offers more connection and routing choices, which can relieve bottlenecks and increase throughput. However, approximate methods such as DuJo do not guarantee an improvement at every FOR value.

With all other inputs fixed, the optimal throughput cannot decrease because every solution feasible at a smaller FOR remains feasible. In Fig.~\ref{fig:plot_test_dual_optimization_vs_grid_rand_varying_availability_and_for}, however, DuJo decreases from $164.27$ to $158.71$ Gbps between $70$ and $90$ degrees. DuJo provides an approximation to the optimal solution. Its greedy matching uses link capacities weighted by Lagrange multipliers, and new candidate links can change the selected matching and routes and shift bottlenecks. The implementation reports the final iterate without retaining the smaller-FOR solution as a fallback, so the returned throughput can decrease even though the feasible set expands. We clarified this distinction and specified that the plotted FOR is the half-angle $\theta$.
